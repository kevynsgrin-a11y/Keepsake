Questions to ask a dying loved one

There is no right list for this, only a gentle one. Ask one question, wait longer than feels comfortable, and let the answer be whatever it is. The goal is not an interview; it is a conversation you will still have when they are gone.

Every guide in this family works the same way: ask slowly, one question at a time, and write down the year you asked. The related guides — questions to ask your grandparents, how to record family stories, and the full guide index — cover the rest of the method.

The questions most people are glad they asked

  • What is the story of your life you would want us to keep telling?
  • What do you remember about your mother and father that we never asked about?
  • What are you proud of that no one ever made a fuss over?
  • Is there anything you have wanted to say and been waiting to be asked?

About the family

  • What do you want the youngest ones to know about where we came from?
  • Is there anything in the family you would want us to make peace with, or let go of?
  • Who should keep what — not the will's version, the story version?
  • What should we do every year to remember you the way you actually were?

The practical ones, saved for the right moment

  • Where are the things only you know — accounts, papers, passwords, the key to the box?
  • Is there anyone you have lost touch with that you would want to hear from?
  • Is there anything you would want written down, in your words, before it gets harder?

What to say when there is no question left

  • Thank you for the specific things — name them.
  • What you taught me that I use without knowing it came from you.
  • That the family will keep the stories. Then keep them.
The one-rule method: their pace, always. Some days one question is too many. Record when you can — with permission — and when you cannot, write down what you remember the same night. There is no fixing this conversation afterward; there is only having had it. The vault keeps what you keep.
