Family reunion questions that unlock the old stories

A reunion is the one room where every version of the family story is present. Ask the questions that make the versions talk to each other.

Most reunion conversations stay in the present — jobs, kids, the food. The older generation is right there, and so are the cousins who remember the other half of every childhood story. The questions below are built for that room: they work because two or three people answer at once and correct each other, which is how the real record gets made. Capture what comes out (see how to record family stories) and file the best ones in the vault while everyone is still arguing about the year.

The openers (ask anyone, anywhere at the reunion)

  • What's your earliest memory of this reunion — the same gathering, whenever it was?
  • Which cousin were you closest to, and what did you two get blamed for?
  • What did the family eat at gatherings when you were young, and who made it?
  • What's a family saying that only we would understand?

For the oldest generation

  • Who is the one relative everyone should know about that nobody talks about anymore?
  • What did our family believe about itself that turned out not to be true?
  • What was the family's hardest year, and how did people show up for each other?
  • If you could ask your own grandparents one thing you never asked, what would it be? (Then ask it of them — see questions to ask your grandparents.)

The table rounds (best with food and more than two people)

  • Same house, same kitchen: who remembers it differently, and what does each version get right?
  • What did Grandma and Grandpa each let you get away with that the other one didn't know?
  • Every family has a story everyone tells wrong. Which one is ours, and what actually happened?
  • What do the youngest ones here not know yet that they should?

The ones that build the record

  • Who in the family kept photos, letters, or papers — and where are they now?
  • What recipes in this room came from someone no longer here, and who still makes them right?
  • What should we ask at the next reunion that we'll wish we'd asked at this one?
The one-rule method: one question per table, asked once, then let the room go. Write down who answered and roughly when — a disagreement between two aunts about a year is itself a family record. Date the session in the heritage calendar; reunions are the natural milestones to hang family stories on.
