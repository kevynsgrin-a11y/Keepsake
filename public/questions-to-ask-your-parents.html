Questions to ask your parents

You have known them your whole life. That is exactly why the best questions are the ones nobody thinks to ask.

Parents are the most documented and least interviewed people in most families. You know their opinions on everything current; you may not know who they were at twenty-five, what they gave up, or what they would say about their life if someone asked slowly. The questions below are grouped by what each set tends to unlock — start with the easy decade and work toward the harder ones. Record the answers (see how to record family stories), and anchor them to dates in the heritage calendar so they keep their place in the family's timeline.

About before you existed

  • What were you like at my age — honestly, not the version you tell at dinners?
  • What did you want to be at twenty, and what happened to that plan?
  • Who was your first love, and how did it end?
  • What did your parents worry about that turned out not to matter?
  • What did you do that Grandpa/Grandma never found out about?

About each other

  • How did you actually meet — the real version, including the parts you usually skip?
  • What was the other one like before kids, when you had time and no money?
  • What did you fight about in the first year of marriage that you laugh about now?
  • Was there a moment you almost didn't stay? What changed it?

About raising you

  • What was I like as a baby that you never told me because it would have gone to my head?
  • What was the hardest year of raising us, and what was happening that we couldn't see?
  • What did you give up that you still think about?
  • What's one thing you were right about that I still haven't admitted?
  • What's one thing you regret about those years?

The ones people save for too late

  • What are you most proud of that no one ever mentions?
  • What do you want us to know about you that isn't in any photo album?
  • If you could keep one story about our family alive after you're gone, which one?
  • What do you still want to do — not the bucket-list version, the real one?
  • Is there anything you want to say to me that you've been waiting for me to ask?
The one-rule method: one question per conversation, not a list across the table. A question asked while driving or cooking gets a truer answer than one asked across an interview. Date each answer in the vault — the year you asked becomes part of the story.
